Carrot and Fennel Velouté with a Hint of Citrus

4 servings · Prep 10 min · Cooking 20 min · Easy

A very sweet and creamy side dish that makes the most of the natural sweetness of carrots and the freshness of fennel. Ideal for introducing little ones to delicate vegetable flavours without using salt.

🌿 Seasonal recipe💰 Save Euro🌱 Vegan🧒 Kid-friendly🌾 Gluten-free🐄 Dairy-free🥛 Lactose-free🇮🇹 Italian

Ingredients

  • Fresh carrots300 g
  • Fennel200 g
  • Strained orange juice30 ml
  • Extra virgin olive oil15 g
  • Water250 ml

Allergens

None of the 14 main allergens in the ingredients.

Method

  1. Begin by thoroughly washing 300g of fresh carrots and 200g of fennel under cold running water to remove any traces of soil. Use a vegetable peeler to remove the outer skin of the carrots and discard the tough, fibrous outer layers of the fennel, perhaps keeping the green fronds for a final garnish. Cut both vegetables into very small cubes, approximately half a centimetre in size, aiming for a uniform shape to ensure even cooking throughout. The result should be clean, neatly diced vegetables ready for the pot; my advice is never to skip peeling the carrots, as the skin can impart a slightly bitter aftertaste to the velvety soup.
  2. Transfer the diced carrots and fennel into a heavy-bottomed stainless steel saucepan and add 250ml of cold water, which should just about cover the vegetables. Place the pan over medium heat and wait for the liquid to reach a boil, without adding any salt to keep the natural flavours pure. You will notice the water starting to take on an orange tint from the carrots as it heats up; ensure the flame is not too high to prevent excessive evaporation of the initial liquid. The chef's trick is starting with cold water to better extract the nutrients and natural sweetness from the vegetable fibres.
  3. Once the water reaches a boil, reduce the heat to the lowest setting and cover the saucepan with a tight-fitting lid to trap the steam. Let it simmer gently for about 20 minutes, checking occasionally to make sure the liquid hasn't dried out completely, though the low heat should prevent this. The vegetables are ready when a carrot cube can be easily crushed against the side of the pan with a fork, offering no resistance at all. The aroma released will be sweet and delicate; do not overcook them, as you want to preserve the vibrancy of the bright orange colour.
  4. Turn off the heat and, using a ladle or a small sieve, remove about half of the remaining cooking water from the pot and transfer it to a separate small bowl. This step is crucial for managing the final density of the soup, allowing you to adjust it gradually without the risk of ending up with a thin, watery broth. You will see that the cooking water has an intense colour and concentrated flavour, making it perfect for reincorporating later. My tip is to keep this reserved water warm so it doesn't lower the temperature of the cream when you come to finish the dish.
  5. Take an immersion blender and lower it directly into the saucepan containing the vegetables and the remaining liquid, starting to blend at medium speed with steady vertical movements. Continue processing the mixture until you achieve a perfectly smooth, silky cream, entirely free from lumps or stray fibres, a process that should take about 2-3 minutes of constant work. The consistency should resemble a fluid purée, and the colour should be a brilliant, inviting orange. For an even more refined texture, you could pass the mixture through a fine-mesh sieve, but if you chopped the vegetables well initially, this shouldn't be necessary.
  6. Now, pour the 30ml of filtered orange juice directly into the still-warm soup and stir well with a wooden spoon or a hand whisk. The acidity and fragrance of the citrus will act as a natural flavour enhancer, compensating for the lack of added salt and bringing an unexpected freshness to the dish. The result will be a cream with a lingering citrus aroma that perfectly balances the sweetness of the carrot and fennel. Be careful not to boil the soup after adding the juice, as this preserves the volatile aromatic notes and the vitamins found in the orange.
  7. Carefully assess the consistency of the resulting cream: if it appears too thick or stays too firmly on the spoon, add one or two tablespoons of the reserved cooking water. Stir thoroughly after each addition until you reach the desired texture, which should be coating and rich but able to slide gently off the spoon. You will achieve a homogenous cream that does not separate on the plate, a sign of a perfect emulsion between the fibres and the liquid. Remember that the soup tends to firm up slightly as it cools, so leave it just a touch more fluid than your ideal serving consistency.
  8. Finish the preparation by adding 15g of extra virgin olive oil while the soup is off the heat, drizzling it in while stirring gently to incorporate it perfectly. The oil not only adds a necessary healthy fat for nutritional balance but also glazes the soup, making it look shiny and professional. Pour the cream into individual shallow bowls and serve it lukewarm, the ideal temperature for fully appreciating the aromatic nuances of the natural ingredients. The secret to an impeccable service is to slightly warm the bowls before pouring the soup, maintaining a constant temperature without overheating the delicate cream.
