Sautéed Fennel with Lemon and Black Pepper

4 servings · Prep 10 min · Cooking 18 min · Easy

When cooked in a pan, fennel loses its overpowering aniseed note and becomes sweet and buttery. The final addition of black pepper and lemon makes it a refined side dish at zero cost.

A light and fragrant side dish that transforms fennel into a tender and aromatic accompaniment. The freshness of lemon pairs beautifully with the bold touch of black pepper and fresh parsley. Perfect for balancing fish or white meat main courses.

Ingredients

  • Fennel800 g
  • Lemon (juice)20 ml
  • Garlic1 clove
  • Parsley5 g
  • Extra virgin olive oil15 ml

Allergens

None of the 14 main allergens in the ingredients.

Method

  1. Begin the preparation by cleaning 800g of fresh fennel bulbs. Using a sharp knife, trim the green feathery fronds, making sure to set them aside in a small bowl for the final garnish, and remove any tough or bruised outer layers. Rinse the bulbs under cold running water to eliminate any grit or soil trapped between the layers. You will then have clean, white fennel hearts ready for processing without waste. Chef's tip: don't discard the outer scraps; use them for a light vegetable stock.
  2. Move on to the technical cutting of the fennel to ensure even cooking. Divide each bulb into vertical quarters and then slice them about 1cm thick, ensuring each slice retains a small piece of the core at the base so they don't fall apart during cooking. This task requires a stable chopping board and a sharp chef's knife to obtain precise, regular sections. The result should be fan-shaped slices, consistent and uniform in size. Chef's tip: the 1cm thickness is the perfect compromise between an outer sear and a tender heart.
  3. Take a large non-stick frying pan and pour in 15ml of extra virgin olive oil along with the 'in camicia' garlic clove (with its skin on), after lightly crushing it with the palm of your hand. Heat over medium heat for about 2 minutes, moving the pan until the garlic starts to sizzle gently, releasing its characteristic aroma without burning. As soon as the garlic becomes lightly golden, you can choose to remove it if you prefer a milder flavour. You will achieve a fragrant fatty base ready to welcome the vegetables. Chef's tip: leaving the skin on the garlic prevents a bitter aftertaste and makes it much more digestible.
  4. Lay the fennel in the pan, trying to distribute them in a single layer to promote the Maillard reaction across the entire surface. Turn the heat up to high and sear them without moving them too much for the first 3-4 minutes, until the bottom appears beautifully golden and caramelised. Use a spatula to gently flip them halfway through so the browning is uniform on both sides of the slices. You will smell a sweet, toasted aroma indicating the correct transformation of the fennel's natural sugars. Chef's tip: do not overcrowd the pan; if necessary, use two pans to prevent the fennel from boiling instead of searing.
  5. Once browned, add about half a glass of water (approx. 100ml) and a pinch of salt to create the necessary moist cooking environment. Lower the heat to medium, immediately cover the pan with a tight-fitting lid, and let it braise for about 10-12 minutes. During this stage, the trapped steam will soften the fennel fibres without losing their essential juices. The fennel should become tender but still offer some resistance when bitten. Chef's tip: check halfway through cooking that the water hasn't completely evaporated to prevent the bottom from burning.
  6. After the braising time, remove the lid and check the consistency of the fennel: if the tip of a knife easily enters the core, they are ready. If too much liquid remains at the bottom, increase the heat slightly for a minute to reduce it, letting the slices sizzle again in the remaining oil. The fennel should appear glossy and slightly translucent, ready for the final acidulation phase. This step is crucial for concentrating the flavours before adding the citrus note. Chef's tip: do not cook them until they become mush; an 'al dente' texture is the hallmark of a professional dish.
  7. Now pour 20ml of fresh lemon juice over the hot fennel in the pan. Maintain a high heat for about 60 seconds, rotating the pan or tossing the fennel so the juice creates a light, silky glaze with the cooking juices. The heat will evaporate the excess acidity of the lemon, leaving only its fresh, aromatic note that perfectly balances the fennel's natural sweetness. You will see the fennel coated in a light, fragrant sauce. Chef's tip: always use fresh lemons rather than bottled juice to avoid metallic or artificial notes.
  8. Turn off the heat and proceed with the final aromatic seasoning directly in the pan. Add 5g of finely chopped fresh parsley and a generous grind of black pepper, preferably freshly ground to release all the spice's essential oils. Stir gently one last time and plate the fennel, garnishing each serving with the green fronds you saved at the beginning. The dish will look vibrant in colour, balanced in taste, and perfect in texture. Chef's tip: freshly ground black pepper makes the difference between an ordinary and a gourmet dish thanks to its aromatic complexity.

Tips

  • Cut the fennel into slices of the same thickness to ensure they cook evenly.
  • Add the lemon juice only at the end of cooking to preserve its fresh aroma and color vibrancy.
  • Store them in an airtight container in the refrigerator for up to two days.

Variations

  • Add whole, lightly crushed garlic cloves for a more intense aroma during the browning process.
  • Sprinkle with finely chopped curly parsley for a more refined and professional presentation.
