Sautéed Swiss Chard with Sultanas and Toasted Pine Nuts

4 servings · Prep 15 min · Cooking 15 min · Easy

A recipe that elevates Swiss chard, often considered less than exciting. The addition of sultanas and pine nuts recalls Italian Renaissance traditions, creating a bittersweet balance.

A classic home-style recipe combining the sweetness of raisins with the crunch of pine nuts. The savory chard is balanced by a light spicy touch, creating a versatile side dish.

Ingredients

  • Swiss chard (large-ribbed variety)1 kg
  • Sultanas30 g
  • Pine nuts20 g
  • Extra virgin olive oil30 ml
  • Dried chilli flakes1 pinch
  • Saltto taste

Allergens

Tree nuts

Method

  1. Start by separating the crisp white stalks from the tender green leaves of 1 kg of Swiss chard using a sharp knife. Cut the stalks into regular pieces of about 2 cm and chop the leaves into wide strips or coarse chunks to ensure even cooking between the two parts. Wash them thoroughly under cool running water to remove any soil residue, then drain them roughly while waiting to cook. My tip is never to discard the stalks: they are the most fibrous part but, when treated correctly, they add a pleasant crunchy and structured note to the dish.
  2. Soak 30 g of sultanas in a small bowl, covering them entirely with lukewarm water for about 10 minutes. This hydration process helps the raisins swell, making them soft, sweet, and juicy before they go into the frying pan for the final stage. Let them rest while you deal with the vegetables until they are well rehydrated and visibly plump to the touch. Once ready, drain them carefully and squeeze them gently between your hands to remove excess water. The secret is to use water that isn't too hot to prevent the raisins from losing their texture and becoming excessively mushy.
  3. Bring a large pot of plenty of salted water to the boil and submerge only the previously cut chard stalks first. Cook them over medium heat for about 5 minutes, as their tough structure takes longer than the green part to soften adequately. Maintain a steady flame to ensure uniform cooking throughout the vegetable fibre without breaking down the exterior. The stalks should be tender when pierced with a fork but still intact and resilient. Do not overdo the salt at this stage, as the flavours will concentrate significantly during the subsequent sautéing phase in the pan.
  4. Add the green leaves to the same pot where the stalks are cooking and continue boiling for another 3-4 minutes at most. Push them down well with a slotted spoon so they are completely submerged in the boiling water right from the start. You will notice the leaves lose volume very rapidly, turning a bright dark green with a yielding texture. The final result should be evenly cooked vegetables, with velvety leaves and perfectly edible stalks. Avoid cooking them too long to prevent excessive loss of precious mineral salts and water-soluble vitamins into the cooking water.
  5. Drain the chard in a fine-mesh colander and let it cool slightly, then squeeze it firmly but gently using your hands or the back of a spoon. This is a fundamental step: removing residual water prevents the dish from being watery or tasting like boiled vegetables during the sautéing phase. You should obtain a compact, very dry vegetable mixture, ready to absorb the fats and aromas of the seasoning. If you leave too much water, the oil won't be able to create the light Maillard reaction necessary for flavour. A professional trick is to quickly pat them with a clean kitchen towel if they still appear too damp.
  6. Heat a large non-stick frying pan over medium heat and pour in 20 g of pine nuts to dry-toast them without adding any oil or fat. Keep the pan moving with rotating motions for about 1-2 minutes until they release an intense resinous aroma and turn uniformly golden. Be very careful during this stage because pine nuts contain natural oils that can burn in seconds, becoming bitter and unpleasant. Once they have reached a beautiful amber colour, transfer them immediately to a cold plate to stop residual cooking. Toasting them separately is the only way to preserve their original crunchiness and toasted aroma.
  7. In the same pan used for the pine nuts, pour 30 ml of extra virgin olive oil and add a pinch of dried chilli flakes. Heat over low heat for about a minute to allow the heat to extract the spicy essential oils, then add the well-squeezed chard and the softened raisins. Increase the heat to high and sauté everything for about 5 minutes, stirring frequently with a wooden spoon to distribute the seasoning well. The chard must literally 'sizzle' to dry out completely, absorbing the character of the oil and the sweetness of the raisins. Remember that high heat is essential to concentrate flavours and create a deep aromatic profile.
  8. Turn off the heat, add the reserved toasted pine nuts, and adjust the salt according to your personal taste. Stir one last time carefully to distribute the pine nuts evenly, taking care to do this only at the end to prevent them from softening with the residual moisture of the vegetables. The dish is ready when it looks shiny, fragrant, and absolutely free of liquids at the bottom of the pan. Serve the chard warm as a refined side dish, perfect for balancing the saltiness of a meat main course or the delicacy of eggs. The final touch of pine nuts added off the heat ensures that contrast of textures that elevates the preparation.

Tips

  • Squeeze the chard well after boiling to prevent it from releasing too much water in the pan.
  • Toast the pine nuts over low heat, being very careful not to burn them as they turn bitter quickly.
  • Use a whole chili pepper if you prefer a milder heat, remembering to remove it before serving.

Variations

  • You can replace the raisins with dried cranberries for a more acidic final note.
  • Add a handful of toasted breadcrumbs at the end of cooking for a crunchy textural contrast.
