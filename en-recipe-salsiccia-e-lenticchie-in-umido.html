Sausage and Lentil Stew

4 servings · Prep 20 min · Cooking 60 min · Hard

A timeless classic of Mediterranean peasant cuisine, where the savouriness of the sausage meets the creaminess of the lentils in a rich and fragrant sauce. This version wisely balances animal and plant proteins for a filling, economical one-pot dish, perfect for family evenings.

A hearty and invigorating one-pot dish, typical of the cold season. The combination of savory browned sausage and the texture of lentils creates a traditional and satisfying blend of flavors.

Ingredients

  • Pork sausage (luganega or nodini type, traditional Italian sausages)400 g
  • Small dried lentils300 g
  • Tomato passata (sieved tomatoes)250 g
  • Carrots100 g
  • Brown onion80 g
  • Celery60 g
  • Extra virgin olive oil30 ml
  • Vegetable stock or water800 ml
  • Bay leaves2 pz
  • Salt and black pepperto taste

Allergens

Celery

Method

  1. Trim and finely chop 100 g of carrots, 60 g of celery, and 80 g of brown onion to create the aromatic base of the dish. Use a sharp knife or a mezzaluna to obtain a homogenous chop of about half a centimetre, ideal for even cooking. Keep the chopped vegetables aside in a small bowl while you prepare the rest of the ingredients and the pot. The result must be a colourful and fresh mix that will serve as the flavourful base for the entire stew. The chef's tip is to avoid the electric mixer so as not to blend the vegetables, thus maintaining a rustic texture that improves the final consistency of the sauce.
  2. Rinse 300 g of dried lentils thoroughly under cold running water, using a fine-mesh sieve. Move the legumes with your fingers to ensure you remove any impurities or small mineral residues common in dried legume packages. Let them drain well for a few minutes so they don't bring too much cold water into the initial browning phase. The lentils should appear shiny and completely clean, ready to absorb the flavours of the seasoning. The chef's tip is not to soak the lentils if they are small, as they would tend to fall apart excessively during long cooking with the meat.
  3. In a large casserole, heat 30 ml of extra virgin olive oil and add the previously prepared chopped carrots, celery, and onion. Turn on the heat to medium-low intensity and let the vegetables soften gently for about 8-10 minutes, stirring occasionally with a wooden spoon. The vegetables should become tender and the onion should be almost transparent without taking on too much colour, releasing a sweet and aromatic scent. This step is fundamental to building the depth of the final flavour. The chef's tip is to add a pinch of salt and a drop of water if the vegetables tend to colour too quickly, thus maintaining the necessary moisture.
  4. Take 400 g of sausage and cut it into pieces about 3-4 centimetres long, leaving them whole or slightly scoring the casing. In a separate non-stick pan, sear the sausage over high heat for 5 minutes, turning it often until it is well browned on all sides. This operation allows you to seal the meat and remove excess fat that will drip onto the bottom of the pan and which you will then discard. You will get tasty morsels of meat with an inviting outer crust and a reduced fat content for a lighter version. The chef's tip is to prick the sausage with a toothpick before browning it to facilitate the escape of the heavier saturated fats.
  5. Join the browned sausage and the clean lentils in the casserole with the now softened vegetable base. Stir energetically over medium heat for about 2 minutes, ensuring that the legumes and the meat flavour each other with the oil and aromas. In this phase, the lentils will undergo a sort of light toasting that will help them maintain their shape during the subsequent stewing. The appearance will be shiny and the scent of the pork will blend harmoniously with that of the vegetable base. The chef's tip is to deglaze with a tablespoon of red wine vinegar or dry white wine in this phase to cleanse the palate and give a balanced note of acidity.
  6. Add 250 g of tomato passata and cover everything with about 800 ml of hot vegetable stock (or water if you prefer a more neutral taste). Insert the 2 bay leaves and bring the temperature of the liquid to a light boil using medium-high heat. Once the first boil is reached, lower the heat to minimum so that the liquid just hints at simmering. The liquid must cover the ingredients by at least two fingers, anticipating absorption by the lentils. The chef's tip is to always heat the liquids before adding them so as not to cause a thermal shock that would make the skin of the lentils tough and difficult.
  7. Cover the casserole with a lid, leaving only a small vent, and continue cooking over low heat for about 40-45 minutes. Check the preparation every 15 minutes, stirring gently from bottom to top to prevent the lentils from sticking to the bottom. If the stew should dry out too quickly, add another ladle of hot stock, keeping the consistency always moist and soft. The result sought is a tender but still intact legume, enveloped by a thick, red, and velvety sauce. The chef's tip is not to add salt during this phase, as sodium can prevent the lentil fibres from softening correctly.
  8. Towards the end of the predicted time, taste a lentil and a piece of sausage to check the cooking and finally adjust the salt and black pepper. If the sauce is too liquid, remove the lid for the last 5 minutes of cooking and slightly raise the heat to encourage the evaporation of excess water. The lentils should have absorbed almost all the stock, transforming into a sort of rich cream that binds the pieces of meat. Turn off the heat when the consistency is thick but still fluid, not excessively dry. The chef's tip is to remove the bay leaves before serving, as they have by now released all their essential oil and could be bothersome in the plate.
  9. Let the stew rest in the covered casserole for at least 10-15 minutes before bringing it to the table. This rest is fundamental so that the temperatures equalise and the flavours of the sausage and lentils merge completely, significantly improving the taste experience. During this time, the starch from the lentils will continue to thicken the cooking base, making the dish even more succulent and creamy. You will notice that the colour will become deeper and the aroma more enveloping and less pungent. The chef's tip is to never skip this step, as stewed lentils are notoriously better when they are not boiling hot and have had time to settle.
  10. Serve the sausages and lentils in individual soup plates, making sure each portion contains a balanced amount of meat and legumes. Complete each dish with a drizzle of raw extra virgin olive oil and, if liked, a sprinkle of freshly ground black pepper. Accompany the dish with slices of crusty bread, possibly wholemeal or rye, lightly toasted to contrast with the softness of the stew. The dish should be presented steaming, colourful and inviting, a true hymn to Italian home cooking. The chef's tip is to keep any leftovers in the refrigerator: the next day, reheated with a drop of water, they will be even tastier thanks to the maturation of the flavours.

Tips

  • Brown the sausage initially to remove excess fat before adding it to the lentils.
  • Use small lentils that hold their shape well during cooking without breaking apart.
  • Season with salt only at the end of cooking to prevent the legume skins from hardening.

Variations

  • Add diced potatoes along with the lentils for an even thicker consistency.
  • Scent the dish with a sprig of rosemary instead of bay leaf for a different aromatic note.
