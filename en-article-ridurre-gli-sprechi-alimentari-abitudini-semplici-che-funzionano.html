Anti-waste

Reducing food waste: simple habits that work

A practical guide to smart pantry management in line with European regulations and food safety.

Introduction: the value of food and the impact of waste

Reducing food waste is not just an ethical or environmental choice, but an act of responsibility that directly affects the household economy and global resource management. According to FAO data, approximately one third of all food produced for human consumption is lost or wasted every year. In the home, much of this phenomenon stems from inefficient shopping habits and, above all, a lack of knowledge regarding storage methods and the wording used on labels. As food technologists, our task is to provide the tools to understand that food which is no longer aesthetically 'perfect' or is approaching a certain date can still be safe and nutritious, provided that specific guidelines from health authorities and European bodies are followed.

Reading labels: the difference between safety and quality

One of the pillars in the fight against waste is the correct interpretation of EU Regulation 1169/2011 regarding the provision of food information to consumers. It is essential to distinguish between the 'Use By' date and the 'Best Before' date (BBE). The Use By date, indicated by the phrase 'use by', is a strict deadline related to food safety. Once this date has passed, the food may pose microbiological risks and must not be consumed. In contrast, the BBE date, indicated by 'best before', refers to organoleptic quality. After this date, the product may undergo slight changes in flavour, texture or colour, but if stored correctly, it remains safe to eat. Training your palate and your eyes to assess a product beyond its BBE date can save dozens of kilograms of food from the bin every year.

Organising the fridge and the FEFO rule

The refrigerator is not just a cold cupboard, but an ecosystem with different temperature zones. Storing food on the wrong shelf accelerates its deterioration. The bottom section (above the vegetable drawers) is the coldest, making it ideal for raw meat and fish; the middle and top shelves are suitable for dairy, eggs and cooked foods. An infallible method borrowed from industrial logistics is FEFO: 'First Expired, First Out'. When putting your shopping away, move existing products to the front and place new ones at the back. This simple action prevents opened or near-expiry packets from being forgotten in the dark corners of the appliance.

Planning and mindful shopping

Waste prevention starts before you even enter the shop. Planning weekly meals is the most effective tool to avoid impulse buying of perishable products that we won't have time to cook. Following a strict shopping list allows you to maintain control over household stocks and reduces the temptation to give in to bulk-buy promotions, which often prove uneconomical if they lead to part of the product being wasted.

Some good habits to adopt during and after shopping include:

  • Always check the pantry and freezer before leaving to avoid duplicates.
  • Buy fresh produce (fruit and vegetables) in quantities appropriate for actual consumption, preferring loose items where possible.
  • Always take thermal bags with you to maintain the cold chain while transporting frozen and chilled goods.
  • Check the integrity of packaging at the time of purchase to ensure the maximum shelf-life promised by the manufacturer.

Creative recovery and managing leftovers

Leftovers should not be considered scraps, but ingredients for new dishes. However, from a food safety perspective, it is essential to manage meal remnants correctly. Cooked food should not remain at room temperature for more than two hours (one hour in summer) to prevent bacterial growth. Once cooled, it should be placed in clean airtight containers and consumed within 2-3 days, or frozen immediately if it is to be used further down the line. Turning stale bread into breadcrumbs or croutons, or using the less 'prime' parts of vegetables for a stock, are circular kitchen practices that value every penny spent and respect the work of producers.

Conclusion

Reducing food waste is a journey made of small daily actions and greater scientific awareness of what we eat. By adopting rational habits in pantry management and respecting storage regulations, we can contribute to a more sustainable food system, while ensuring maximum safety for our health and concrete savings for our pockets.
