Panko and Rosemary Pork Milanese Cutlets

4 servings · Prep 20 min · Cooking 10 min · Easy

An affordable version of the classic Milanese cutlet, using pork loin instead of veal. The 'panko' style breading (obtained by coarsely crushing dry bread) guarantees an unbeatable crunch.

A reinterpretation of the classic cutlet using coarsely grated stale bread for superior crunchiness. Fresh rosemary infuses the pork loin with an irresistible aromatic scent, making this dish ideal for a family Sunday lunch.

Ingredients

  • Pork loin slices4 pcs
  • Medium eggs2 pcs
  • Coarsely grated stale bread120 g
  • Fresh rosemary1 rametto
  • Plain flour (Type 0 flour)30 g
  • Vegetable oil for frying100 ml
  • Saltq.b.

Allergens

Gluten, Eggs

Method

  1. Begin by preparing the 4 pork loin slices: make small incisions on the edges with a sharp knife to prevent the meat from curling in the pan. Place each slice between two sheets of baking parchment and flatten it with a meat mallet using a motion from the centre outwards until it doubles its surface area. Spend about one minute per slice to achieve a uniform thickness of just a few millimetres. The result will be a thin, tender base ready for breading. Chef's tip: do not strike with excessive force to avoid tearing the meat fibres; maintain a firm but fluid touch.
  2. Prepare the seasoning by finely chopping the needles from a sprig of fresh rosemary on a clean cutting board. In a large flat plate, mix the 120g of coarse breadcrumbs with the minced rosemary, distributing it evenly. This operation takes only a couple of minutes and will immediately release the essential oils of the herb. You will get a fragrant, grainy mix that guarantees superior crunch compared to fine breadcrumbs. Chef's tip: use home-grated bread with large holes for an effect similar to Japanese panko.
  3. Organise your workstation with three separate plates for a perfect coating: one with 30g of plain flour, one with the 2 medium eggs (beaten and salted), and the last with the rosemary breadcrumbs. Arrange the plates in this order from left to right to create an efficient assembly line. Beat the eggs with a fork for about 30 seconds until the yolk and white are perfectly combined and slightly frothy. This setup will allow you to work cleanly and without waste. Chef's tip: keep one hand for dry ingredients and the other for the egg to avoid breading your fingers.
  4. Proceed with the first coating stage by dredging each meat slice in the flour, ensuring both sides are covered. Give the meat a firm tap to shake off any excess; only an imperceptible film should remain. This step serves to seal the meat juices and help the egg adhere better. The slice should appear matte and dry to the touch. Chef's tip: flour is the 'primer' of your schnitzel; if you use too much, the breading will slide off during cooking.
  5. Dip the floured slice into the beaten egg mixture, making sure every millimetre of the surface is coated. Lift the meat and let it drip for a few seconds over the plate to remove superfluous egg. This stage takes only a few moments but is crucial for the crust's integrity. The meat should appear shiny and uniformly moist. Chef's tip: if you notice any dry spots, dip the slice in the egg again, as the breadcrumbs will not stick where moisture is missing.
  6. Place the meat in the plate with the breadcrumbs and press firmly with the palm of your hand to make the granules adhere. Flip the slice and repeat, insisting on the edges to seal them perfectly. Apply constant pressure for about 20-30 seconds on each side of the cutlet. The final result should be a thick, compact breading without any gaps. Chef's tip: do not be afraid to press hard; the more the bread is embedded in the fibres, the less likely it is to detach during frying.
  7. Arrange the breaded cutlets on a tray and let them rest in the fridge for 10 minutes before cooking. This resting time allows the flour starch and egg proteins to stabilise, creating a strong bond with the breadcrumbs. During this time, the coating will dry slightly, becoming more stable. You will achieve a crust that doesn't crumble and stays attached to the meat like a second skin. Chef's tip: never skip this step; it is the secret to the classic air 'bubble' that forms between meat and breading.
  8. Heat the 100ml of vegetable oil in a wide frying pan over medium-high heat until it reaches 170°C. If you don't have a thermometer, dip the tip of a toothpick: if it sizzles immediately, the oil is ready. Lay one cutlet at a time to avoid a sudden drop in fat temperature. Cook for about 3 minutes per side, maintaining constant and brisk heat. The cutlet should take on a deep hazelnut colour and an extremely crunchy texture. Chef's tip: do not flip the meat continuously; once per side is enough for perfect browning.
  9. Drain the cutlet from the oil using tongs and place it immediately on kitchen paper to remove excess grease. Let it rest for 30 seconds before adding a pinch of salt to the surface to preserve the crunch. Serve immediately with lemon wedges or a fresh salad to balance the saltiness and the fatty element. The scent of toasted rosemary will be the indicator of the dish's success. Chef's tip: salt the meat only at the end, otherwise the salt will draw moisture out, making the breading soggy.

Tips

  • Make small cuts on the edges of the meat to prevent it from curling while frying in the pan.
  • Press the breading firmly with your palm to ensure it sticks perfectly and doesn't fall off during cooking.
  • Keep the oil temperature constant to achieve even browning while keeping the meat juicy on the inside.

Variations

  • Replace the stale bread with crushed hazelnuts for a more toasted and gourmet breading flavor.
  • Add some grated lemon zest to the beaten eggs for a citrusy note that balances the meat's richness.
