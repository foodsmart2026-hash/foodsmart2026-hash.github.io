Allergens and cross-contamination: how to avoid them in the kitchen

A practical guide to allergen management and cross-contamination prevention for a safe and inclusive kitchen.

Food safety is not just a matter of microbiological hygiene or correct temperature control. Today, one of the most complex and delicate challenges for those working in the food industry – both professionally and at home – is allergen management. According to data from EFSA (European Food Safety Authority), food allergies affect a significant percentage of the population, and the only true therapy is the strict exclusion of the offending ingredient from the diet. In this context, the role of the food preparer becomes fundamental: it is not enough to know the ingredients of a recipe; one must master the processes that prevent an unintended substance from ending up on a sensitive consumer's plate.

The 14 main allergens and EU Regulation 1169/2011

To navigate the kitchen safely, the starting point is EU Regulation No. 1169/2011, which identifies 14 substances or products that cause allergies or intolerances. These must be clearly indicated in the information provided to consumers, both on packaged products and in restaurant menus. The list includes: cereals containing gluten, crustaceans, eggs, fish, peanuts, soya, milk, nuts, celery, mustard, sesame seeds, sulphur dioxide and sulphites (in concentrations higher than 10 mg/kg or mg/l), lupin, and molluscs. Knowing these categories is the first step towards conscious risk management.

What is cross-contamination?

Cross-contamination occurs when an allergen is unintentionally transferred from a food, surface, or utensil to a food that originally did not contain it. This phenomenon can happen in two ways: direct or indirect. Direct contamination occurs when an allergenic ingredient comes into contact with another food (for example, a piece of cheese falling into a salad intended for someone who is lactose intolerant). Indirect contamination, which is more insidious, occurs via vehicles such as unwashed hands, chopping boards, knives, crockery, or even frying oil previously used for other foods.

Operational strategies to prevent risk

Prevention requires a systematic approach that starts from storage and goes all the way to table service. Segregating spaces and tools is the most effective strategy. Here are some essential best practices to adopt in the kitchen:

  • Wash your hands thoroughly with soap and water before starting any preparation and every time you switch food types.
  • Use dedicated utensils for 'allergen-free' dishes. The use of colour-coding (e.g., purple chopping boards for allergens) is a recommended practice in professional kitchens.
  • Clean and sanitise work surfaces between preparations, removing all visible food residue.
  • Store allergenic ingredients in airtight, well-labelled containers, ideally separate from other foods (e.g., on different shelves or in dedicated areas).
  • Do not use the same frying oil or boiling water for different foods (e.g., do not cook gluten-free pasta in water that has been used for durum wheat pasta).
  • Be mindful of airborne contaminants, such as flour, which can disperse and settle on other preparations.

The importance of labelling and communication

Carefully reading product labels is essential. Besides the ingredients list, pay attention to precautionary statements like 'may contain traces of...'. These indicate that the manufacturer cannot guarantee the total absence of an allergen due to potential cross-contamination at the production site. In the kitchen, communication is equally vital: front-of-house and kitchen staff must exchange precise information regarding allergies reported by customers, avoiding guesswork. Transparency is the most powerful tool for building trust and ensuring the health of those at the table.

Conclusions

Managing allergens and avoiding cross-contamination is not just a regulatory requirement, but an act of responsibility and professionalism. By adopting rigorous procedures, continuous training, and constant attention to detail, it is possible to transform the kitchen into a safe environment for everyone, where dietary inclusivity becomes synonymous with quality and gastronomic expertise.
