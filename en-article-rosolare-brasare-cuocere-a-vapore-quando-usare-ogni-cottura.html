Cooking techniques

Searing, braising, steaming: when to use each cooking method

Culinary techniques and chemistry: a practical guide to choosing the ideal cooking method and making the most of every ingredient.

In the kitchen, the difference between a mediocre dish and an excellent gastronomic experience often lies in the mastery of heat. Cooking is not simply heating food, but triggering a series of complex chemical and physical transformations that modify its structure, flavour, and the bioavailability of nutrients. Understanding the science behind searing, braising, and steaming allows you to choose the most suitable method for every raw material, respecting its organoleptic characteristics and ensuring maximum food safety, in line with the most advanced technological guidelines.

Searing: the art of the Maillard Reaction

Searing is a dry-heat cooking technique that involves exposing food to high temperatures, generally between 140°C and 180°C. From a food technology perspective, this process is dominated by the famous Maillard Reaction: an interaction between amino acids and reducing sugars that generates hundreds of new aromatic molecules and the characteristic brown colour. It is a common misconception that searing 'seals' the juices inside the meat; in reality, the crust that forms is permeable, but its primary function is to build a complex and deep aromatic base.

This technique is ideal for tender, thin cuts of meat, for shellfish, and for certain vegetables such as Brussels sprouts or mushrooms. For correct searing, it is essential to dry the surface of the food thoroughly: any residual moisture would absorb energy to evaporate, keeping the temperature stalled at 100°C and preventing the formation of the desired aromatic compounds. A fat with an appropriate smoke point (such as extra virgin olive oil or clarified butter) is the indispensable thermal vehicle to ensure uniformity and prevent carbonisation, which would be toxic as well as unpleasant.

Braising: the transformation of collagen

Braising is a 'mixed' technique that begins with dry searing and continues with slow, prolonged cooking in a moist, confined environment (usually a pot with a lid). It is the method of choice for less expensive cuts of meat that are rich in connective tissue. Science teaches us here that, at constant and moderate temperatures (around 85-95°C), collagen — the tough protein that makes meat chewy — undergoes thermal denaturation, transforming into gelatin. This process makes the muscle fibres soft and succulent.

During braising, the cooking liquid (wine, stock, or jus) acts as a solvent for the aromas and helps maintain constant humidity, preventing the fibres from drying out. It is fundamental never to reach a violent boil, which would make the muscle protein tough and stringy. The result is a perfect balance between the savouriness derived from the initial searing and the tenderness obtained from the slow hydrolysis of the connective tissue. It is a technique that requires patience, but from a technological point of view, it guarantees the maximum aromatic yield from ingredients that would otherwise be inedible.

Steaming: integrity and nutrition

While searing and braising focus on creating new flavours, steaming aims to preserve existing ones. It is a moist-heat technique in which the food does not come into contact with water but is heated by the latent heat released by the water vapour condensing on its surface. This method is particularly valued for its ability to keep water-soluble vitamins (such as vitamin C and B-group vitamins) and minerals intact, which would otherwise be lost in the boiling liquid.

Steam is ideal for vegetables, delicate fish, and Asian steamed breads. By maintaining temperatures close to 100°C, the degradation of plant cell walls occurs in a controlled manner, preserving the 'al dente' texture and the bright colour of natural pigments like chlorophyll. Furthermore, as it does not require the addition of fats during cooking, it allows for better management of the lipid profile of the finished dish, in accordance with the principles of a balanced diet promoted by health authorities.

Choosing the right technique: a practical summary

To decide which method to use, it is necessary to analyse the composition of the ingredient and the desired end result. Here are some guiding criteria based on food technology:

  • Choose searing for ingredients low in connective tissue (fillets, chicken breasts, scallops) where you seek aromatic intensity and contrasting textures.
  • Choose braising for cuts rich in collagen (cheek, shank, shoulder) that need time to become tender and to develop thick sauces.
  • Choose steaming for high-quality ingredients that do not require aromatic correction (asparagus, broccoli, white fish fillets) and whose nutritional value you wish to preserve.
  • Remember that food safety requires reaching specific core temperatures (e.g., 75°C for poultry) regardless of the chosen technique.

Conclusion

The kitchen is a laboratory where the physics of heat meets the biology of ingredients. Knowing how to distinguish between the radiant energy of a sear and the gentle convective heat of steam is not just an exercise for chefs, but a tool for anyone who wishes to consume tastier, safer, and more nutritious food. Experimenting with these techniques means learning to engage with the raw material, respecting its true nature.
