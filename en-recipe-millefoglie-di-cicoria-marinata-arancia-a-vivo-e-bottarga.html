Marinated chicory, orange segments and bottarga mille-feuille

Sardegna · 4 servings · Prep 20 min · Cooking 0 min · Easy

A fresh and refined starter that plays on the contrast between the bitterness of raw chicory, the sweetness of the orange and the saltiness of the bottarga.

🌿 Seasonal recipe🌾 Gluten-free🐄 Dairy-free🥛 Lactose-free🇮🇹 Italian

Ingredients

  • Tender Catalogna chicory400g
  • Blonde oranges2
  • Whole or grated mullet bottarga40g
  • Extra virgin olive oil4 tablespoons
  • Lemon juice1 tablespoon
  • Cider vinegar1 tablespoon
  • Fine saltto taste
  • Black peppercornsto taste

Allergens

Fish, Sulphites

Method

  1. Start by taking 400g of chicory (catalogna) and selecting only the central hearts, which are the lightest and most tender parts of the head. Discard the green, fibrous outer leaves, then rinse the hearts in plenty of cold water to remove any traces of soil or impurities. Dry them with extreme care using a salad spinner or a clean tea towel; any remaining moisture will prevent the dressing from adhering properly. You should achieve perfectly dry and crisp leaves, ready to be prepared. Chef's tip: do not throw away the discarded outer leaves; sauté them in a pan with garlic, oil, and chilli for a quick and tasty side dish.
  2. Now proceed with the technical cut of the chicory: place the hearts on the cutting board and, using a well-sharpened chef's knife, slice them into very thin strips lengthwise. Aim to obtain almost transparent filaments which, thanks to the longitudinal cut, will naturally tend to curl. This step is essential to break the chicory fibres and make it pleasant to the palate despite its naturally tough texture. You will obtain a sort of nest of bright, pale, and very elegant filaments. Chef's tip: if you want an even more dramatic curly effect, submerge the sliced chicory in ice water for 5 minutes, but remember to dry it perfectly again.
  3. Prepare the emulsion for the marinade by pouring 4 tablespoons of extra virgin olive oil, 1 tablespoon of lemon juice, 1 tablespoon of apple cider vinegar, and a pinch of fine salt into a small bowl. Whisk the liquids vigorously with a small whisk or a fork for about a minute until they have bonded into an opaque, slightly thick, and glossy sauce. The acidity of the lemon and vinegar serves to balance the bitter note of the chicory and the intense saltiness of the bottarga. The result must be a homogeneous, velvety, and well-balanced dressing. Chef's tip: always add the salt to the acidic liquids (lemon and vinegar) before pouring in the oil, so it dissolves perfectly without leaving unpleasant grains at the bottom.
  4. Transfer the chicory into a large bowl and pour over the freshly prepared emulsion, distributing it evenly over all the vegetables. Gently massage the leaves with clean hands for about a minute, ensuring every single filament is coated in the dressing; this manual process helps to soften the vegetable fibres. Let everything rest at room temperature for exactly 10 minutes to allow the marinade to penetrate and work. At the end, the chicory will have lost its initial rigidity but will maintain a perfect crunch. Chef's tip: do not exceed 10 minutes of marinating, otherwise the acid will start to over-cook the fibres, making the chicory soft and causing it to lose its beautiful bright colour.
  5. While the chicory is resting, prepare the 2 oranges by peeling them 'a vivo' (segmenting) to extract only the heart. With a sharp, straight-bladed knife, cut off both ends of the fruit to create a stable base, then follow the curve of the orange to remove the peel and all the white fibrous pith (the albedo). Once peeled, cut precisely between the thin membranes to extract only the clean and intact segments of pulp, free of any skin. You should obtain shiny, juicy orange fillets totally free of the white part, which would be bitter to the taste. Chef's tip: perform this operation over a small bowl to collect the juice that naturally escapes; you can use it to reinforce the chicory dressing.
  6. Take the 40g of mullet bottarga and prepare it for finishing the dish with extreme precision. If you have the whole piece, gently remove the protective outer skin and slice it into very thin shavings using a truffle slicer or a very sharp straight-bladed knife. If using pre-grated bottarga, ensure it is of superior quality, but whole shavings provide a much better texture and elegance. The slices should be almost transparent so they can melt harmoniously in the mouth along with the other ingredients. Chef's tip: if the bottarga is too hard or dry, keep it at room temperature for half an hour before slicing to prevent it from crumbling under the blade.
  7. It is time to assemble the 4 portions: take large flat plates and place a generous handful of marinated chicory in the centre of each. Distribute the filaments to form an airy and tall base, avoiding pressing them onto the bottom of the plate to maintain lightness. Try to give volume to the composition, arranging the chicory curls upwards to create a three-dimensional effect. The result should be a bright, inviting, pale green bed occupying the heart of the plate. Chef's tip: if you want a more geometric gourmet plating, use a wide ring mould, but remove it immediately after inserting the chicory to let the curls open up naturally.
  8. Complete your millefeuille by laying the previously prepared orange segments and bottarga shavings over the chicory. Alternate the pieces, tucking them between the vegetable curls to create different levels of colour and flavour in every bite. Divide the ingredients equally among the 4 plates, aiming for a visual balance between the bright orange of the fruit and the amber gold of the bottarga. The dish must appear layered, rich, and very carefully presented, with all elements clearly visible. Chef's tip: do not crowd the ingredients too much; leaving some visual space between each shaving of bottarga will make the dish much more refined.
  9. Conclude the preparation with a generous grind of black peppercorns, distributing it over the entire surface to add an aromatic and spicy note. If there is any dressing left in the chicory bowl, drizzle a few drops around the composition as a final decoration. Serve immediately to preserve the maximum crunchiness of the chicory and the play of contrasts between the saltiness of the sea and the sweetness of the citrus. The final aroma should be a fresh and stimulating mix of citrus and sea breeze. Chef's tip: for a professional touch, serve this millefeuille on plates previously kept in the fridge; this will keep the chicory incredibly crunchy until the very last bite.
