Labels and dates

'Use by' and 'best before': how to read food dates

A practical guide to reading labels: food safety and the conscious fight against waste.

Every year, tonnes of perfectly edible food end up in the bin due to a misunderstanding of labels. In a global context where sustainability and the fight against food waste have become urgent priorities, understanding the difference between the phrases found on packaging is the first step towards responsible consumption. It is not just about saving money, but an act of awareness that combines public health protection with safeguarding the environment.

In Italy and throughout the European Union, food labelling is strictly governed by EU Regulation No. 1169/2011. This legislation clearly distinguishes between two often-confused concepts: the actual 'use by' date and the best-before date. Confusing one with the other can lead to health risks or, conversely, an unnecessary waste of resources.

The 'use by' date: a non-negotiable limit for safety

The phrase 'Use by' (followed by day, month, and possibly year) represents the actual expiry date. This indication is mandatory for food products that are highly perishable from a microbiological point of view, which could constitute an immediate danger to human health after a short period. We are talking about fresh foods such as fresh milk, eggs, meat, fish, and deli products with a short shelf-life.

Once this date has passed, the food is no longer considered safe. Bacterial growth (often invisible to the naked eye and not always accompanied by bad smells) can make the product harmful. According to health guidelines, the sale of products that have passed their 'use by' date is prohibited, and consumers are strongly urged not to eat them, even if they still look fine. In this case, strictness is synonymous with prudence and health protection.

The Best-Before Date: when quality precedes safety

The nature of the phrase 'Best before' is very different. In this case, we are dealing with the best-before date. It applies to more stable products, such as dried pasta, rice, preserves, biscuits, or oils. This date does not indicate the moment the food becomes dangerous, but rather the period until which the producer guarantees the food will maintain its organoleptic and specific properties (fragrance, flavour, colour, texture).

If stored correctly, these foods can be consumed even after the date shown. EFSA (European Food Safety Authority) has highlighted how a correct understanding of the 'best before' date can drastically reduce household waste. A packet of pasta consumed a month after its best-before date might have lost some of its firmness when cooked, but it does not represent a microbiological risk for a healthy individual.

The importance of domestic storage

It is essential to remember that both date indications are only valid if the storage instructions on the packaging have been followed. A carton of fresh milk left out of the fridge for hours will expire well before the printed date. Likewise, an oil exposed to light and heat will go rancid long before its best-before date.

To best manage your pantry and fridge, here are some good practices suggested by food technologists:

  • Apply the FIFO (First In, First Out) method: place more recently purchased products behind those already present, so you consume those closest to their date first.
  • Always check the integrity of the packaging: a dented tin or a lost vacuum seal invalidates the date shown.
  • Respect the fridge zones: the temperature is not uniform; meat and fish should go on the bottom shelf (the coldest), while fruit and vegetables go in the dedicated drawers.
  • Read post-opening instructions carefully: often a product that lasts months sealed must be consumed within 3-4 days once opened.

Beyond the date: how to act to reduce waste

When faced with a product that has passed its best-before date, there is no need to instinctively throw it away. You can perform a sensory self-assessment: check for mould, bulging packaging, or unpleasant smells. If the food looks fine, it can be consumed, perhaps by using it in recipes that involve cooking, which further stabilises its profile.

In conclusion, the key to smart kitchen management lies in attentiveness. Reading the label carefully is not just a bureaucratic act, but a form of respect towards food and oneself. Distinguishing between safety (use by) and quality (best before) allows us to eat safely, save money, and make a tangible contribution to the planet's sustainability.
