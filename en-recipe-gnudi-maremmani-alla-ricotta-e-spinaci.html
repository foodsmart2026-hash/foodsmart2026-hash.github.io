Spinach and Ricotta Gnudi

4 servings · Prep 20 min · Cooking 15 min · Medium

A Tuscan classic 'gnudi' (naked), which is essentially the ravioli filling served without the pasta shell. Light, budget-friendly and very quick to prepare using fridge staples.

🌿 Seasonal recipe💰 Save Euro🇮🇹 Italian

Ingredients

  • frozen spinach600 g
  • cow's milk ricotta (soft Italian whey cheese)250 g
  • large eggs1 pcs
  • grated Parmigiano Reggiano (hard aged Italian cheese)60 g
  • 00 flour (finely milled Italian flour)50 g
  • butter40 g
  • fresh sage4 foglie
  • nutmeg1 pizzico

Allergens

Gluten, Eggs, Milk

Method

  1. Start by cooking 600g of frozen spinach in a large pan with a splash of water and a pinch of salt over a medium heat. Let them wilt for about 8-10 minutes, stirring occasionally with a wooden spoon until they are completely tender and the excess liquid has evaporated. Once cooked, transfer them to a colander and let them cool slightly so they can be handled. You should aim for well-cooked greens that still retain a vibrant colour. Chef's tip: do not over-salt at this stage, as the Parmigiano and the final dressing will provide plenty of savouriness.
  2. This is the most crucial step for the success of the dish: take the lukewarm spinach and squeeze it with extreme force using a potato ricer or a clean tea towel. You must apply enough pressure to eliminate every trace of residual moisture; not a single drop of water should come out, ensuring the dough stays firm without needing too much flour. Once squeezed, finely chop the spinach with a knife on a wooden board. You will obtain a very dry, dense mass of greens with an intense aroma. The chef's secret for perfect gnudi is the absolute absence of moisture in the spinach.
  3. In a large bowl, combine the chopped spinach with 250g of well-drained cow's milk ricotta, 1 large egg, and a pinch of freshly grated nutmeg. Use a fork to blend the ingredients with circular motions, pressing any ricotta lumps against the sides of the bowl. Work the mixture for about 2-3 minutes until it is chromatically uniform and free of white streaks. The result should be a firm, fragrant, and velvety cream. Chef's tip: use high-quality ricotta and let it drain in a sieve for at least an hour before use.
  4. Add 60g of grated Parmigiano Reggiano to the mixture and continue stirring with the fork to incorporate it perfectly. The final consistency should be malleable, similar to a dense mousse that holds its shape when lifted. Ensure the cheese is evenly distributed to guarantee a balanced flavour in every bite. You will have a dark green dough, speckled with the white of the cheese and very aromatic. The chef's advice is not to overdo the nutmeg: it should be a background aroma and must not overpower the delicate flavour of the ricotta.
  5. Prepare a tray with 50g of plain flour and start shaping the gnudi by taking about 20-25g of dough at a time. Shape walnut-sized balls by gently rolling them between the palms of your hands, aiming for a regular spherical shape. Coat each ball in the flour, shaking it between your hands to remove the excess; they should only have an almost invisible protective veil. The gnudi should appear slightly powdery but remain soft to the touch. My tip is to keep your hands slightly damp if the mixture becomes too sticky during shaping.
  6. Place a large pot of salted water on the hob and bring it to a rolling boil. Once it reaches the boil, lower the heat slightly to prevent violent bubbles from breaking the gnudi during immersion. Gently drop the gnudi into the water, a few at a time so as not to drop the temperature too sharply. Cook them for about 2-3 minutes; they will be ready the moment they float to the surface, just like classic potato gnocchi. Chef's tip: test one gnudo first to check the consistency; if it falls apart, add a teaspoon of flour to the rest of the mixture.
  7. While the gnudi are cooking, prepare the sauce in a large frying pan by melting 40g of butter with 4 fresh sage leaves over a very low heat. Let the butter foam slightly and the sage release its essential oils, infusing an earthy and enveloping scent. The butter should turn a light hazelnut colour without burning or turning dark. You will get a golden and fragrant emulsion, ready to embrace the delicacy of the ricotta. The chef's tip is to use fresh rather than dried sage for maximum aromatic intensity.
  8. Drain the gnudi as soon as they float using a slotted spoon and transfer them directly into the pan with the butter and sage. Turn the heat up slightly and move the pan gently (or use a silicone spoon) to coat each gnudo with the melted butter. Let them infuse for less than a minute, adding a tablespoon of pasta water if necessary to make the sauce creamy and glossy. Serve the gnudi immediately while hot, finishing with an extra dusting of Parmigiano if desired. The chef's secret is gentleness in this final phase: do not toss them too vigorously to avoid ruining their characteristic softness.
